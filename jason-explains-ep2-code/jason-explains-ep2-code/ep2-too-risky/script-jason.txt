Now, part two of the fear: what if I pick the wrong one? Fair question — because if you put all your money in ONE company, and that company goes bust, you can lose everything. It has happened. Big names, gone. That is not a small risk. That is a huge risk. But here's the trick — you don't have to buy one company. An index fund buys hundreds of them at once. The S&P 500 is 500 of the biggest companies in America, all in one basket. Buy the index, and you own a tiny slice of all 500. So if one company goes bust? That's one out of 500 — about 0.2% of your money. It stings. It doesn't sink you. [pause] Think of it like eggs. One egg, dropped — breakfast ruined. A basket of 500 eggs, one breaks — you still have breakfast. Same money. Completely different risk. That's the whole idea of an index fund: you're not betting on one horse. You're betting on the whole racecourse. And the racecourse has been running for a hundred years.

And then there's the biggest lever of all: time. Here's the number that changed how I think about risk. In any single year, the market goes up about 73% of the time. Roughly three years out of four, you win. But stretch your holding period to 20 years — and in almost a hundred years of records, there has never been a single 20-year stretch where the market lost money. Not one. The worst 20-year stretch still made about 2 to 3% a year. The best made almost 18% a year. [pause] Now — that does not mean the wait is comfortable. After the 2008 crash, the market fell 57% — and it took about five and a half years just to get back to where it started. After the COVID crash in 2020, it fell 34% in about a month — and it was back at new highs in five months. Same market. One wait was five months. The other was five years. You can't know which one you're getting. [pause] That's the real risk. Not that the market never recovers — it always has, so far. The risk is that you need the money before it does. So the question isn't really "are stocks risky?" The question is "when do I need this money?"
